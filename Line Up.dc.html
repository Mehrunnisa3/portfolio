Line Up

An all-in-one sports drop-in activity finder for Surrey residents — find sessions, see who’s going, and show up with confidence.

THE PROBLEM

Finding recreational activities in Surrey means navigating a fragmented landscape. Rec centre drop-ins, nearby gyms, fitness classes, and local leagues all live in different places — separate websites, Facebook groups, and word of mouth. Nothing brings all of this together. And even when residents do find something, the information available may be rarely enough to commit. How many people are actually going? What skill level are they? Will there be enough players for a real game? For someone new to Surrey or fitting sport into a busy schedule, that uncertainty is often enough to stop them going altogether.

Surrey.ca — the city's primary recreation portal — lists drop-in sessions but was built as a general municipal platform covering everything from property tax to waste collection and so on. Recreation is one section among many, and the experience reflects that. Knowing just about the remaining spots tells you nothing about whether the session will have enough players, what skill level they play at, or whether showing up alone means you will get to play at all.

This gap is not just a UX problem. For newcomers and immigrants building a life in a new place, recreational sport is one of the fastest pathways to community and belonging. When the system fails to surface that opportunity clearly, the people who need it most are left out.

THE PROCESS

01. Research Process

Problem identification and competitive audit

I began with my own experience — arriving in Surrey as an athlete, unable to find indoor courts in winter, discovering Surrey.ca through word of mouth two years after moving, and showing up alone to sessions not knowing if enough players would be there. This personal experience became the foundation of the research.

A competitive audit of five platforms — Surrey.ca, Meetup, Facebook Groups, Strava, and Sportiner — confirmed that no existing tool combined session discovery, attendance visibility, and player connection in one place for Surrey recreational sports.

User research

To better understand the experiences of Surrey residents and validate the problem identified through competitive analysis, a questionnaire was distributed to 7 Surrey residents who play recreational sports. In additon to the  primary research, 17 posts from r/SurreyBC were analysed — documenting the same frustrations independently raised by strangers over years, confirming the problem extends beyond the study participants.

Data points synthesised

76 data points were synthesised through affinity mapping, producing four clusters that shaped every design decision in Line Up.

Affinity Mapping

Key research findings

1
Skill level uncertainty

4 of 7 participants independently raised skill level uncertainty as a primary barrier. Experienced players avoided sessions where amateurs showed up. Beginners feared being unwelcome at competitive sessions. Neither group had any way to assess this before arriving.

2
Attendance visibility

The spots remaining format on Surrey.ca created anxiety rather than confidence. Participants who only had one or two evenings per week to play could not afford a wasted trip based on a meaningless number.

3
Discovery gap

Most participants discovered recreational activities through word of mouth not through any platform. Several Reddit posts spanning four years show the same questions being asked repeatedly — confirming that Surrey.ca's recreation section is effectively invisible to new residents.

4
Player connection

For partner-dependent sports like badminton, showing up alone meant potentially not getting to play at all. Multiple participants had avoided sessions entirely because they had no one to go with.

Persona

Marilyn : 28,  graduate student, South Surrey. New to Surrey, relies on classmates to find activities, has heard of Surrey.ca but never used it. Plays basketball and volleyball. Core frustration,missing out on activities she does not know exist, and wasting time showing up to sessions that are not worth attending.

“When I want to be active after work, I want to quickly find a basketball session near me where I know the skill level matches mine and there will be enough players — so I can just show up, play, and feel like my time was well spent.”

02. From Wireframes to UI

User flows

Three user flows were mapped before any screens were designed.

1 Flow 1 covers finding and booking a drop-in session — the core journey.
2 Flow 2 covers gym and fitness class discovery.
3 Flow 3 covers player profiles and open-for-pairing across the attendees screen.

Wireframes

12 lo-fi wireframes were built in Figma before any visual design began. Every layout decision was traced back to a research finding — the attendance bar format, the price placement, the “who’s going” section, the open-for-pairing prompt at booking confirmation.

Design system

A design system was established before touching hi-fi screens. Plus Jakarta Sans for display headings. Inter for all body text. A 10-colour palette anchored by Midnight Navy and Electric Blue. An 8-component library including the session card in three states — good to go, filling up, and session full.

Hi-fi screens

The final prototype covers the complete user journey from onboarding through booking confirmation. Key design decisions include the attendance bar showing 14/20 rather than spots remaining, the confidence signal indicating whether a session has enough players, price visible upfront in the session hero block, and the open-for-pairing tag set at booking confirmation rather than during onboarding.

03. Usability Testing

Methodology

A remote unmoderated usability test was conducted with 7 participants using the System Usability Scale and five post-task questions. Participants were Surrey residents who play recreational sports. The core task given to all participants was to find a basketball drop-in session, assess whether it was worth attending, and complete a booking.

Testing was conducted remotely via questionnaire and voice note explanation. Results were analysed using thematic analysis across four clusters mirroring the original research methodology.

SUS results

Line Up achieved an average SUS score of 87.14 out of 100 — Grade B, Excellent — significantly above the industry benchmark of 68. Six of seven participants scored in the excellent to best-imaginable range. One participant scored 62.5, identified as a session discoverability gap for users less familiar with the app structure.

Thematic analysis findings

Attendance and confidence — 6 of 7 participants confirmed the attendance bar gave them confidence to book. Participants specifically mentioned that knowing 18 out of 20 spots were filled meant their time would not be wasted.

Navigation and flow — 5 of 7 found navigation clear and efficient. One participant completed the full booking flow in under 3 minutes. One participant could not find sessions at all, identifying a discoverability gap for less familiar users.

Social features and player connection — 5 of 7 found the “who’s going” feature and open-for-pairing tags genuinely useful. Two participants independently requested in-app partner request functionality — a strong signal for future iteration.

Perceived value over existing solutions — 6 of 7 compared Line Up favourably to their current discovery methods. The consolidation of location, timing, price, skill level, and attendance in one place was the most consistently mentioned advantage.

Iterations

Five design changes were made based on testing findings. The most significant were surfacing waitlist availability directly on the session detail screen, replacing icon-only sport filters with labelled pills, merging the “who’s going” and attendees sections, and adding skill level to each player row on the attendees screen.

04. Reflection

Line Up validated that the attendance confidence problem is real and that a focused, research-backed solution can score significantly above industry usability benchmarks even at prototype stage.

If I were to continue this project the immediate next step would be implementing partner request functionality — two participants independently requested this without prompting, suggesting the open-for-pairing tag meets awareness needs but leaves users wanting a more direct way to connect before sessions.

A moderated usability test would strengthen the findings. Testing was conducted remotely without observation, which means friction points not captured through self-reporting may exist. One participant’s inability to find sessions in the unmoderated test is the clearest example — a moderated session would have revealed exactly where the discovery breakdown occurred.

If Line Up were to ship, the metrics I would track are booking completion rate, return usage rate within 7 days, and whether the open-for-pairing feature correlates with reduced session abandonment — specifically whether users who see compatible pairing options are more likely to complete their booking.

This project reinforced that the strongest design decisions are the ones you can trace directly back to something a real person said. Every feature in Line Up exists because someone in Surrey — in a survey, in a Reddit post, or in my own experience — encountered a problem that no existing platform was solving.
